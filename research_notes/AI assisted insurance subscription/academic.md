# Academic Literature on Human–AI Collaboration for AI Assistants in Insurance Sales and Underwriting (front-office subscription flow)

Note on sourcing: items marked [verified] had bibliographic details checked against a web source this session. Items marked [from memory] are canonical works whose citation is given from the researcher's knowledge; DOIs are believed correct but should be spot-checked before external publication. Where a detail is uncertain this is stated explicitly.

## Q1. Levels of automation and adaptive autonomy: when to suggest vs when to act

### Takeaway
The classic human-factors literature says automation should be set per function (information acquisition, analysis, decision selection, action implementation). It supports high automation for gathering and analysing information, and caution for automating decisions and actions when they are costly or irreversible, because failures at high levels of automation are the most severe (the "lumberjack effect"). 2025 agent frameworks restate this as the user's role (operator > collaborator > consultant > approver > observer), with approval checkpoints placed before irreversible or binding actions. For a subscription flow this points to: the AI pre-fills and checks data autonomously, recommends products and options, and the agent explicitly approves anything that binds the insurer or customer (quote issuance, signature, binding, payment).

### Cited Findings
- **Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000)**, "A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation," *IEEE Trans. Systems, Man, and Cybernetics – Part A*, 30(3), 286–297, DOI 10.1109/3468.844354 [from memory]. The paper splits automation into four stages (information acquisition, information analysis, decision/action selection, action implementation), each of which can sit anywhere from low to high automation. It recommends choosing a level per stage by weighing the main criteria (mental workload, situation awareness, complacency, skill degradation) against the secondary ones (automation reliability, cost of decision/action consequences). High automation of decision selection is advised only when reliability is high and consequences are low. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354)
- The 10-level scale (from "computer offers no assistance" up to "computer decides everything and ignores the human") comes from **Sheridan & Verplank (1978)**, MIT Man-Machine Systems Lab technical report [from memory]. Level 5, "executes the suggestion if the human approves," corresponds to an approval checkpoint; level 6, "allows the human a restricted time to veto before automatic execution," corresponds to a veto window. — cited via [Parasuraman et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354)
- **Onnasch, Wickens, Li & Manzey (2014)**, "Human performance consequences of stages and levels of automation: an integrated meta-analysis," *Human Factors* 56(3), 476–488, DOI 10.1177/0018720813501549 [from memory]. Higher degrees of automation improve routine performance and lower workload, but they reduce situation awareness and make performance much worse when the automation fails (the "lumberjack" effect: the higher the automation, the harder the fall). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720813501549)
- **Endsley & Kiris (1995)**, "The out-of-the-loop performance problem and level of control in automation," *Human Factors* 37(2), 381–394, DOI 10.1518/001872095779064555 [from memory]. Full automation lowered situation awareness and slowed recovery after failures; intermediate levels kept operators in the loop. **Endsley (2017)**, "From here to autonomy: lessons learned from human–automation research," *Human Factors* 59(1), 5–27, DOI 10.1177/0018720816681350 [from memory]. Endsley describes an "automation conundrum": the more autonomous and reliable a system becomes, the less likely the human is to notice and handle its failures. — [Endsley & Kiris](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779064555); [Endsley 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720816681350)
- **Bainbridge (1983)**, "Ironies of automation," *Automatica* 19(6), 775–779, DOI 10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 [from memory]. Automating routine work leaves humans with the hardest exception cases while also taking away the practice that keeps their skills up. This matters for agents whose routine subscriptions become automated while they still have to handle complex risks. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)
- **Kaber & Endsley (2004)**, *Theoretical Issues in Ergonomics Science* 5(2), 113–153, DOI 10.1080/1463922021000054335 [from memory]. They tested adaptive automation, where the level of automation changes with workload or context, and found benefits when control is periodically handed back to the human. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/1463922021000054335)
- **Shneiderman (2020)**, "Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence: Reliable, Safe & Trustworthy," *Int. J. Human–Computer Interaction* 36(6), 495–504, DOI 10.1080/10447318.2020.1741118. The book version is **Shneiderman (2022)**, *Human-Centered AI*, Oxford University Press [from memory]. Shneiderman rejects the one-dimensional "levels of automation" scale and proposes a 2-D framework in which systems can achieve high human control *and* high computer automation together. He argues for designs that keep humans in control, especially in consequential, life-critical or rapid-action domains, and gives examples such as preview of actions, undo, audit trails, and "supervisory control." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2020.1741118)
- **Horvitz (1999)**, "Principles of mixed-initiative user interfaces," *Proc. CHI '99*, 159–166, DOI 10.1145/302979.303030 [from memory]. Horvitz proposes deciding whether to act, ask, or do nothing from the expected utility of the action given the system's uncertainty about the user's goal. The rule is to act automatically only when confidence is high and the cost of being wrong is low, to ask for clarification when it is uncertain, and to scale the precision of the service to its uncertainty. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/302979.303030)
- **Feng, McDonald & Zhang (2025)**, "Levels of Autonomy for AI Agents," arXiv:2506.12469 [verified, preprint]. The paper treats autonomy as a design choice separate from capability, and defines five levels by the user's role: operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, observer. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.12469)
- **Mozannar, Bansal, Fourney et al. / Microsoft Research (2025)**, "Magentic-UI: Towards Human-in-the-loop Agentic Systems," arXiv:2507.22358 [verified, preprint; exact first author not verified]. It proposes concrete agent-oversight mechanisms: co-planning (the user edits the plan before execution), co-tasking, action guards (approval required before irreversible actions, with user-configurable frequency), multi-tasking, and long-term memory, run in a sandboxed executor. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22358); [MSR blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/magentic-ui-an-experimental-human-centered-web-agent)
- Several 2026 arXiv preprints build on the levels framework (unreviewed; listed for awareness only). One argues that autonomy levels determine where checkpoints sit and who is accountable in regulated contexts ([arXiv 2605.12105](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12105)). Another separates "allowed autonomy level" (permission) from capability ([arXiv 2607.23438](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.23438)). A third, the "2026 Singapore Consensus," lists in-the-loop approval, on-the-loop monitoring and batch review as oversight modes, and argues that approval requirements can be relaxed as an agent demonstrates reliability ([arXiv 2608.14611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.14611)). — details from search snippets only.
- Industry research outside peer review: **Handa et al. (2025)**, "Which economic tasks are performed with AI? Evidence from millions of Claude conversations," arXiv:2503.04761 (Anthropic Economic Index) [from memory]. It reports that a majority of observed use was augmentative rather than automative (about 57% vs 43% in the first release; the figure is from memory and should be verified). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04761)

### Inferences
- Map the subscription flow onto Parasuraman's four stages:
  - **(1) Data capture and enrichment** (prefill from customer record or documents, address and vehicle lookups): high automation is acceptable, with visible provenance.
  - **(2) Analysis** (risk flags, eligibility checks, gaps in cover): high automation, shown as highlights.
  - **(3) Decision selection** (product, cover level, deductible, accept/refer/decline): suggest only (Sheridan level 3–4). Offer alternatives, not a single answer.
  - **(4) Action** (issue quote, bind, e-sign, payment, sending documents to the customer): approval-gated (level 5), never silent. A veto window (level 6) is acceptable only for low-consequence, reversible steps.
- The lumberjack and out-of-the-loop findings argue against fully automating routine cases while still expecting agents to catch rare errors. Interleaving periodic active engagement, such as asking the agent to confirm key risk facts, keeps situation awareness up.
- Horvitz's expected-utility rule gives an operational policy: act only when model confidence × reversibility is high; ask when the system is uncertain about intent; stay silent otherwise.

### Gaps
- None of the levels-of-automation studies above were conducted in insurance distribution; transfer from aviation, process control and medicine is by analogy.
- The 2025–2026 agent-autonomy literature is mostly preprints with little controlled empirical evaluation of approval-checkpoint frequency (rubber-stamping vs fatigue). I found no peer-reviewed study that quantifies optimal checkpoint density for business agents.

## Q2. Trust calibration, automation bias, over-reliance, algorithm aversion: interventions that reduce over-reliance

### Takeaway
People both under-rely on algorithms (algorithm aversion, especially after seeing them err) and over-rely on them (automation bias, appreciation). The goal is *appropriate* reliance, not maximal trust. Human+AI teams often fail to beat the better of the two alone, especially in decision tasks: a 2024 meta-analysis found worse-than-best-alone performance on average. The interventions with the best evidence are:
- cognitive forcing, such as having the human decide first or adding a delay, which works but is disliked;
- explanations that genuinely lower the cost of verification;
- calibrated confidence;
- giving humans some control or modification ability, which reduces aversion.

### Cited Findings
- **Lee & See (2004)**, "Trust in automation: designing for appropriate reliance," *Human Factors* 46(1), 50–80, DOI 10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392 [from memory]. The foundational framing: trust should be *calibrated* to the automation's actual capability, and both over-trust (misuse) and under-trust (disuse) are failures. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392)
- **Parasuraman & Manzey (2010)**, "Complacency and bias in human use of automation: an attentional integration," *Human Factors* 52(3), 381–410, DOI 10.1177/0018720810376055 [from memory]. Automation bias produces both omission errors (missing problems the aid does not flag) and commission errors (following wrong advice). It occurs in experts as well as novices and is not removed by training alone. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)
- **Skitka, Mosier & Burdick (1999)**, "Does automation bias decision-making?" *Int. J. Human-Computer Studies* 51(5), 991–1006, DOI 10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252 [from memory]. Participants with an automated aid made both omission and commission errors that non-aided participants avoided. **Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt (2012)**, *JAMIA* 19(1), 121–127, DOI 10.1136/amiajnl-2011-000089 [from memory], systematically reviewed automation bias in clinical decision support. — [Skitka](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252); [Goddard](https://doi.org/10.1136/amiajnl-2011-000089)
- **Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015)**, "Algorithm aversion: people erroneously avoid algorithms after seeing them err," *J. Experimental Psychology: General* 144(1), 114–126, DOI 10.1037/xge0000033 [from memory]. People lost confidence in an algorithm more quickly than in a human after seeing the same mistakes. **Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2018)**, "Overcoming algorithm aversion: people will use imperfect algorithms if they can (even slightly) modify them," *Management Science* 64(3), 1155–1170, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643 [from memory]. Letting people adjust algorithmic forecasts even slightly greatly increased their use of the algorithm. — [2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033); [2018](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643)
- **Logg, Minson & Moore (2019)**, "Algorithm appreciation: people prefer algorithmic to human judgment," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 151, 90–103, DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005 [from memory]. Lay people weighted identical advice more when told it came from an algorithm. Experts, such as forecasters, discounted advice more, which hurt their accuracy. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005)
- **Bansal, Wu, Zhou, Fok, Nushi, Kamar, Ribeiro & Weld (2021)**, "Does the whole exceed its parts? The effect of AI explanations on complementary team performance," *Proc. CHI '21*, DOI 10.1145/3411764.3445717 [from memory]. When human and AI had comparable accuracy, explanations increased acceptance of AI recommendations *whether or not they were correct*. Explanations did not produce complementary performance beyond what showing confidence alone achieved. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445717)
- **Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos (2021)**, "To trust or to think: cognitive forcing functions can reduce overreliance on AI in AI-assisted decision-making," *Proc. ACM HCI* 5(CSCW1), Art. 188, DOI 10.1145/3449287 [from memory]. Cognitive forcing designs reduced over-reliance compared with simple explainable-AI designs. The three designs were having the person decide before seeing the AI, a slow-down/wait, and showing the AI only on demand. However, participants rated the most effective designs least favourably (a trade-off between effectiveness and acceptance), and the benefit was larger for people high in Need for Cognition, which raises an equity concern. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449287)
- **Vasconcelos, Jörke, Grunde-McLaughlin, Gerstenberg, Bernstein & Krishna (2023)**, "Explanations can reduce overreliance on AI systems during decision-making," *Proc. ACM HCI* 7(CSCW1), DOI 10.1145/3579605 [verified]. In a cost-benefit framework (5 studies, N=731, maze task), people strategically choose whether to engage with explanations. Explanations reduce over-reliance when they lower the cost of verifying the AI relative to the effort of the task. Task difficulty, explanation difficulty and monetary stakes all moved over-reliance. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06823); [Stanford](https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/paper.php?id=472)
- **Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024)**, "When combinations of humans and AI are useful: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *Nature Human Behaviour* 8, 2293–2303, DOI 10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1 [study verified; volume/pages/DOI from memory]. The meta-analysis covered 106 experiments and 370 effect sizes from 2020–2023. On average, human–AI combinations performed worse than the best of human or AI alone (Hedges' g = −0.23). Losses concentrated in *decision* tasks, while gains appeared in *content creation* tasks. When the human was better than the AI alone, combining them helped; when the AI was better, combining them hurt. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/)
- **Fügener, Grahl, Gupta & Ketter (2021)**, "Will humans-in-the-loop become borgs? Merits and pitfalls of working with AI," *MIS Quarterly* 45(3), 1527–1556, DOI 10.25300/MISQ/2021/16553 [from memory]. AI advice made human decisions converge, losing the unique human knowledge that could complement the AI. Teams worked better when the AI delegated uncertain cases to humans than when humans delegated to the AI, because humans are poor at knowing when to delegate. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.25300/MISQ/2021/16553)
- **Schemmer, Kuehl, Benz, Bartos & Satzger (2023)**, "Appropriate reliance on AI advice: conceptualization and the effect of explanations," *Proc. IUI '23*, DOI 10.1145/3581641.3584066 [from memory]. The paper defines appropriate reliance as two measures: relative positive AI reliance (switching to the AI when it is right) and relative positive self-reliance (keeping one's own answer when the AI is wrong). This is a usable metric for evaluating a copilot. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3581641.3584066)
- **Green (2022)**, "The flaws of policies requiring human oversight of government algorithms," *Computer Law & Security Review* 45, 105681, DOI 10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681 [from memory]. Green's review finds that people are frequently unable to perform the oversight functions policies assign them: they cannot reliably detect errors or override appropriately. "Human in the loop" can therefore legitimise flawed systems unless oversight effectiveness is empirically validated. This is an open debate directly relevant to "agent validates" designs. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681)
- Generative-AI era: **Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch, Drosos, Rintel, Banks & Wilson (2025)**, "The impact of generative AI on critical thinking: self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence effects from a survey of knowledge workers," *Proc. CHI '25*, DOI 10.1145/3706598.3713778 [from memory; DOI should be checked]. Among 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more. Critical effort shifted toward verification and "stewardship" of AI output. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- Industry evidence from insurance (non-academic, Swiss Re): people frequently accept inaccurate AI recommendations without identifying errors (automation bias) in life and health underwriting and claims, which Swiss Re cites as the reason for human oversight. — [Swiss Re](https://www.swissre.com/reinsurance/insights/thriving-with-ai.html)

### Inferences
- Agents in a subscription flow are domain experts with partial unique knowledge, such as what the customer said, local context and visual inspection. Vaccaro et al. and Fügener et al. suggest the design should *elicit that unique knowledge* (structured "anything the system doesn't know?" prompts) rather than just ask the agent to approve the AI's answer.
- Use cognitive forcing *selectively*, for high-stakes decisions only (declines, referrals, unusual risk acceptance, cover exclusions): ask for the agent's own assessment before revealing the AI recommendation. Applying it everywhere will meet resistance (Buçinca's acceptance trade-off).
- Let agents edit AI-generated values (Dietvorst 2018): adjustable suggestions increase uptake and reduce aversion.
- Measure appropriate reliance, meaning override accuracy and acceptance-when-right (Schemmer 2023), not acceptance rate. A high acceptance rate is not evidence of success.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that measures automation bias specifically among insurance sales agents or underwriters using GenAI copilots.
- Effects of cognitive forcing on time-pressured sales conversations, with the customer present, are untested. The lab studies use non-expert crowdworkers.

## Q3. Explainability for decision support and confidence display

### Takeaway
Explanations are not automatically helpful: feature-importance style local explanations often *increase* blind acceptance. Explanations work when they are contrastive, selective and social (Miller), answer the user's actual question (Liao's question bank), reduce verification cost (Vasconcelos), and include organisational or social context (Ehsan's social transparency). Confidence displays can calibrate trust if the confidence is itself well calibrated. For LLMs, expressed uncertainty, especially first-person hedging, reduces over-reliance, while users systematically overestimate LLM accuracy when uncertainty is not conveyed.

### Cited Findings
- **Miller (2019)**, "Explanation in artificial intelligence: insights from the social sciences," *Artificial Intelligence* 267, 1–38, DOI 10.1016/j.artint.2018.07.007 [from memory]. Human explanations are contrastive ("why P rather than Q"), selected (a few causes, not all), and social (an interaction). Probabilities matter less than causes. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2018.07.007)
- **Lai & Tan (2019)**, "On human predictions with explanations and predictions of machine learning models: a case study on deception detection," *Proc. FAT* '19*, 29–38, DOI 10.1145/3287560.3287590 [from memory]. The paper describes a spectrum of machine assistance from explanations only to explicit predictions with accuracy statements. More explicit assistance improved human accuracy but also increased reliance, which raises an agency vs performance trade-off. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3287560.3287590)
- **Lai, Chen, Liao, Smith-Renner & Tan (2023)**, "Towards a science of human-AI decision making: an overview of design space in empirical human-subject studies," *Proc. FAccT '23*, DOI 10.1145/3593013.3594087 [from memory]. This survey of more than 100 studies finds heterogeneous tasks and measures, with mixed results for explanations; it is useful as a map of the evidence. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3593013.3594087)
- **Liao, Gruen & Miller (2020)**, "Questioning the AI: informing design practices for explainable AI user experiences," *Proc. CHI '20*, DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376590 [from memory]. The XAI Question Bank is organised by user question types (How, Why, Why not, What if, How to be that, How to still be this, Input/data, Output, Performance), derived from interviews with 20 UX practitioners on 16 AI products. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376590)
- **Ehsan, Liao, Muller, Riedl & Weisz (2021)**, "Expanding explainability: towards social transparency in AI systems," *Proc. CHI '21*, DOI 10.1145/3411764.3445188 [from memory]. Showing past decisions by colleagues on similar cases (the "4W": who, what, when, why) helped sellers in an enterprise *sales pricing* scenario calibrate trust in the AI recommendation. This is the closest XAI study to an insurance sales context. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445188)
- **Zhang, Liao & Bellamy (2020)**, "Effect of confidence and explanation on accuracy and trust calibration in AI-assisted decision making," *Proc. FAT* '20*, DOI 10.1145/3351095.3372852 [verified]. Confidence scores helped calibrate trust; local explanations did not. Calibration alone does not improve joint accuracy unless the human brings complementary knowledge. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.02114)
- **Chen, Liao, Vaughan & Bansal (2023)**, "Understanding the role of human intuition on reliance in human-AI decision-making with explanations," *Proc. ACM HCI* 7(CSCW2), DOI 10.1145/3610219 [from memory]. Example-based explanations, unlike feature-based ones, helped people use their own intuition to detect AI errors. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3610219)
- **Kim, Liao, Vorvoreanu, Ballard & Wortman Vaughan (2024)**, "'I'm not sure, but...': examining the impact of large language models' uncertainty expression on user reliance and trust," *Proc. FAccT '24*, DOI 10.1145/3630106.3658941 [from memory]. First-person uncertainty expressions ("I'm not sure, but…") reduced over-reliance on incorrect LLM answers and increased accuracy, with a modest reduction in trust. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3630106.3658941)
- **Steyvers, Tejeda, Kumar, Belem, Karny, Hu, Mayer & Smyth (2025)**, "What large language models know and what people think they know," *Nature Machine Intelligence* 7, 221–231, DOI 10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7 [from memory]. Users overestimate LLM accuracy from default explanations (a "calibration gap"), and longer explanations raise confidence even when they add no accuracy. Aligning the expressed uncertainty with the model's internal confidence narrowed the gap. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7)

### Inferences
- For subscription, prioritise answering the agent's actual questions from the question bank:
  - "Why this product or tariff rather than that one?" (contrastive)
  - "What would change the price or eligibility?" (what-if, counterfactual)
  - "What data did you use, and where did it come from?" (input provenance)
  - "How reliable is this check?" (performance)
- Use example- and precedent-based explanations ("similar risks accepted or referred by underwriting last quarter"), following Ehsan's social transparency and Chen et al. These suit expert sellers better than SHAP-style bars.
- Display confidence only if it is calibrated, and express it in words and action, for example "verify this field: low confidence from scanned document." Avoid long, fluent justifications, which inflate trust (Steyvers 2025).

### Gaps
- No insurance-specific XAI user study was found for front-office agents. Ehsan et al.'s sales-pricing scenario is the nearest analogue.
- Evidence on numeric versus verbal confidence for LLM outputs in professional workflows is still thin and mostly lab-based.

## Q4. Guidelines and empirical studies of generative / agentic AI with humans in the loop

### Takeaway
Amershi et al.'s 18 guidelines remain the standard design checklist. Field evidence on GenAI copilots in service work is consistently positive on productivity, with the largest gains for novices: about 15% in a call centre, and about 34% for the least experienced agents. The "jagged frontier" result warns that on tasks just outside the AI's competence, users who rely on it do markedly worse. For agentic systems (2024–2026), the emerging consensus is plan preview and editing, action guards before irreversible steps, and graduated autonomy, but rigorous peer-reviewed evaluations are still scarce.

### Cited Findings
- **Amershi, Weld, Vorvoreanu, Fourney, Nushi, Collisson, Suh, Iqbal, Bennett, Inkpen, Teevan, Kikin-Gil & Horvitz (2019)**, "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction," *Proc. CHI '19*, DOI 10.1145/3290605.3300233 [from memory]. The paper sets out 18 guidelines in four phases:
  - **Initially:** G1 make clear what the system can do; G2 make clear how well it can do it.
  - **During interaction:** G3 time services based on context; G4 show contextually relevant information; G5 match social norms; G6 mitigate social biases.
  - **When wrong:** G7 support efficient invocation; G8 support efficient dismissal; G9 support efficient correction; G10 scope services when in doubt; G11 make clear why the system did what it did.
  - **Over time:** G12 remember recent interactions; G13 learn from behaviour; G14 update and adapt cautiously; G15 encourage granular feedback; G16 convey consequences of user actions; G17 provide global controls; G18 notify users about changes.
  The guidelines were validated with 49 design practitioners on 20 AI products. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300233)
- **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025)**, "Generative AI at Work," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 140(2), 889–942, DOI 10.1093/qje/qjae044 [verified; one CV lists pp. 889–944]. A staggered rollout of a GPT-based assistant to about 5,000 customer-support agents raised issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average (14% in the earlier NBER/arXiv version). Gains were about 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal effect on the most experienced; secondary sources report the tool may slightly reduce quality for top workers. The AI appears to spread the tacit best practices of top performers, and the study also found improved customer sentiment and lower attrition (from memory). — [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11771v1); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2304.11771.html)
- **Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick, Lifshitz-Assaf, Kellogg, Rajendran, Krayer, Candelon & Lakhani (2023)**, "Navigating the jagged technological frontier: field experimental evidence of the effects of AI on knowledge worker productivity and quality," HBS Working Paper 24-013, SSRN 4573321 [from memory]. It has since been published as the lead article of *Organization Science* (March–April 2026 issue), DOI 10.1287/orsc.2025.21838 [issue and DOI seen in AOM announcement; author list in the published version not verified]. In an experiment with 758 BCG consultants and GPT-4, tasks inside the frontier showed 12.2% more tasks completed, 25.1% faster, and over 40% higher quality. On a task outside the frontier, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions (figures from memory of the WP). The paper describes "centaur" (dividing work) and "cyborg" (intertwining) collaboration patterns. — [AOM announcement](https://ent.aom.org/discussion/march-april-2026-issue-of-organization-science-3); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4573321)
- **Noy & Zhang (2023)**, "Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence," *Science* 381(6654), 187–192, DOI 10.1126/science.adh2586 [from memory]. ChatGPT cut professional writing time by about 40% and raised quality by about 18%, and it compressed inequality between workers. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586)
- **Dell'Acqua et al. (2025)**, "The Cybernetic Teammate: a field experiment on generative AI reshaping teamwork and expertise," HBS Working Paper 25-043 / NBER [from memory]. In a study with 776 P&G professionals, individuals with AI matched the performance of two-person teams without AI and spent about 16% less time (time figure seen only in a secondary source). — [secondary mention](https://insuranceagencyinsights.substack.com/p/the-cybernetic-teammate-a-field-experiment/comments)
- **Fang, Yuan, Zhang, Donati & Sarvary**, "Generative AI and firm (sales) productivity: field experiments in online retail," CESifo Working Paper 2026 / arXiv:2510.12049 [verified as WP]. Randomised experiments on GenAI tools across a retailer's sales funnel found treatment effects of 0–16.3%, depending on how much each tool added beyond existing practice; gains came mainly through conversion. — [CESifo](https://www.ifo.de/en/cesifo/publications/2026/working-paper/generative-ai-and-sales-productivity-field-experiments-online); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12049)
- **Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (2025)**, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: evidence from high school mathematics," *PNAS* 122(26), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122 [from memory]. Unrestricted GPT-4 access raised practice performance but *lowered* later unaided performance; a tutor-style version with guardrails mitigated the harm. This matters for novice agents' skill development. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
- Agent oversight mechanisms: Magentic-UI (Microsoft, 2025), as described under Q1 (co-planning, action guards before irreversible actions, user-configurable approval frequency). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22358)
- **Kellogg, Valentine & Christin (2020)**, "Algorithms at work: the new contested terrain of control," *Academy of Management Annals* 14(1), 366–410, DOI 10.5465/annals.2018.0174 [from memory]. Algorithmic direction, evaluation and discipline of workers provoke "algoactivism" and resistance. This is relevant if the copilot is perceived as surveillance of sales agents. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/annals.2018.0174)

### Inferences
- Amershi's G2 (how well), G9 (efficient correction), G10 (scope when in doubt) and G16 (convey consequences) map directly onto a subscription copilot. In particular, the AI should hand off to underwriting rather than guess on out-of-appetite risks.
- The Brynjolfsson and Noy & Zhang pattern (novices benefit most) suggests the copilot will be most valuable for new or occasional agents and for complex products. Experienced agents need efficient dismissal (G8) to avoid a quality drag.
- The jagged frontier implies the copilot should explicitly signal when a case sits outside its competence: unusual risks, non-standard clauses, regulatory edge cases.
- Bastani et al. suggest a "coach" mode for trainees, so that new agents build product and underwriting knowledge rather than only copying suggestions.

### Gaps
- I found no field experiment of GenAI copilots specifically with insurance sales agents or front-office underwriters. Evidence must be transferred from customer support, consulting and retail.
- Peer-reviewed CHI/CSCW empirical studies of *approval checkpoints* in business agents (2024–2026) were not located in this pass. Magentic-UI is a technical report; the levels-of-autonomy work is preprint.

## Q5. Insurance-specific academic work: AI in underwriting and distribution, robo-advice, intermediaries, fairness

### Takeaway
The insurance literature is strong on value-chain impacts and on fairness and discrimination in pricing, especially *indirect/proxy* discrimination, but thin on human–AI interaction for agents and underwriters. Robo-advice research shows real benefits (diversification) alongside persistent algorithm aversion, with only about 40% delegation even when the algorithm is clearly superior. Discrimination-free pricing methods and the EU AI Act's classification of life/health insurance pricing and risk assessment as high-risk make human oversight and explainability legal as well as UX concerns.

### Cited Findings
- **Eling, Nuessle & Staubli (2022)**, "The impact of artificial intelligence along the insurance value chain and on the insurability of risks," *Geneva Papers on Risk and Insurance – Issues and Practice* 47(2), 205–241, DOI 10.1057/s41288-020-00201-7 [verified]. The review drew on 91 papers and 22 industry studies. AI lowers costs and creates new revenue, and the business model is expected to shift from loss compensation to loss prediction and prevention. Insurability effects run both ways: more accurate loss probabilities, but some risks become high-severity/low-frequency. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/gpprii/v47y2022i2d10.1057_s41288-020-00201-7.html)

### Inferences
- In a subscription copilot, any AI-suggested pricing or eligibility element should rely on approved tariff/underwriting rules rather than free LLM inference, to avoid introducing proxy discrimination (Lindholm et al.). Free-text customer information captured by the LLM, such as names or health hints, should not flow into rating features without governance.
- The EU AI Act's Art. 14 explicitly names automation bias. Oversight UI choices (Q2 interventions, logging of overrides) are therefore compliance evidence for life/health lines, not only good UX.
- Robo-advice findings suggest the agent-plus-AI hybrid is likely more acceptable to customers than pure automation. However, AI help is most valuable for less sophisticated cases and clients (D'Acunto et al.), which parallels the novice-worker result.

### Gaps
- No PhD thesis on AI assistants in insurance *distribution* (agents généraux, courtiers) was located via the searches run; theses.fr was not directly searchable from this environment. Recommend direct theses.fr / ProQuest queries ("intelligence artificielle" + "distribution d'assurance"; "insurance agent" + "decision support" dissertation).
- No peer-reviewed empirical study was found of GenAI copilots used by insurance agents during subscription or sale. Insurance-specific HCI evidence is a clear gap in the literature.
- Robo-advice evidence is from investment advice; transfer to insurance product advice (IDD duty-to-advise, demands-and-needs tests) is by analogy.
- Several 2026 items (Organization Science author list, arXiv agent-governance papers) were seen only as snippets and announcements.
